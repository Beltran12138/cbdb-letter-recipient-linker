Linking letter titles to recipients in CBDB

An automatic first pass for 致書 / 答書 records: given a letter's writer and title, propose the recipient's CBDB person ID with a calibrated confidence, and abstain when unsure.

Data: CBDB SQLite 2026-10-03 and three earlier releasesCode: cbdb-letter-recipient-linker (MIT; disamb/predict.py links new letters)

Summary

Trained only on what CBDB contained on 2022-07-27, the linker was asked to identify the 9,738 titled letters that entered CBDB between 2022-07 and 2023-03 (the CSA Ming letters batch). It named the correct recipient first for 87.1% of them. When it answered only at confidence ≥ 0.9, it covered 62.2% of the batch at 97.4% precision; across 10 repetitions with different candidate order, folds and model seed, that was 58–66% at 97.4–97.9%.

The gain is concentrated in letters that human contributors also found easy. On the 704 letters that were still unidentified in the public CSA task file and were identified later, top-1 accuracy falls to 24.0%. The CSA page lists 54,391 Ming letters, of which only a minority have identified addressees. How many of the rest are easy cases is unknown, so a small reviewed pilot would be the next honest step.

The task

CBDB holds 14,958 letter relations (codes 429 致書Y, 431 答Y書, 433 致Y啓, 435 答Y啓), each with a title in c_text_title and an identified recipient. Writers are mostly Ming (9,728) and Song (4,535). The recipient is usually named by courtesy name, style name or office, rarely by personal name:

Method

  1. Candidates. Substrings of the title (2–4 characters) are looked up against every person's name and alternate names (ALTNAME_DATA), with common suffixes such as 山人 and 先生 stripped and a short table of variant characters (荅/答, 廵/巡, 菴/庵, 内/內 and others). Four rules add candidates the dictionary misses: surname followed within seven characters by a given name; surname plus an office term checked against POSTED_TO_OFFICE_DATA; the writer's kin named in the title; and the stripped-suffix aliases. Candidates more than 150 years from the writer are dropped.
  2. Ranking. A gradient-boosted classifier scores each candidate on 27 features: match length, whether the preceding character is the candidate's surname, alias type (字, 號, 謚號…), how many people share the matched string, distance in index year, shared dynasty and address, non-letter associations and kinship with the writer, and offices whose names appear in the title.
  3. Confidence and abstention. Confidence is the model's probability for its top candidate. Below a fixed threshold of 0.9, the linker returns no answer.
  4. Leakage guard. No feature uses letter relations (codes 429–436), since these are the labels.

  • run1 and run2 used a confidence normalised over the candidate list. That cannot express "none of these", which explains run2's low precision on hard letters; run3 and run4 use the raw probability.
  • run3's test set is dominated by records the method cannot address: 289 of 536 have the title [n/a], and 253 are Republican-era letters. Its threshold rule (lowest threshold reaching 90% training precision) also selected 0.010; run4 replaced it with a 97% rule before running.
  • Correction, 2026-10-07. The first version of this note reported run4 at the 97% rule's threshold (0.945): 51.1% answered at 97.7%. A re-run from a clean copy showed that rule is unstable. Over 10 repetitions it chose thresholds from 0.94 to 1.0 and answered 0–54% of the batch, while precision when answering stayed at 97.8–98.2%. Results also depended on PYTHONHASHSEED through a set iteration in candidate generation, now fixed. Figures above use the fixed 0.9 threshold, which was pre-registered as secondary; run1–run3 rows are unchanged.
  • On run4's training folds, letters scored 0.9–1.0 had a mean probability of 0.96 and were correct 96% of the time (n = 1,951).
  • Simple baseline on the run1 split (dictionary + era + surname, answer only when one candidate remains): answered 37.9% at 89.6% precision.

Limits to weigh

  • Selection. Every gold label is a letter that people managed to identify. Letters that resisted identification are under-represented, so the headline figures are an upper bound for any remaining backlog.
  • Rule design. The four candidate rules were designed after reading about 45 training examples from the 2026 release, some of which belong to the run4 batch. The model and threshold never saw that batch.
  • Alias provenance. An alias added to CBDB while identifying a letter would favour the dictionary. In run4, only 2 of the 8,757 test letters that matched a 2022-07 alias had that alias sourced from the same collection.
  • No expert review. Correctness is measured only against CBDB's own identifications. Disagreements have not been examined by a specialist, and some may be errors in the gold labels.

Questions for the CBDB team

  1. The CSA page lists 54,391 Ming letters with addressees for a minority. Would you be open to a pilot: we run the linker on a few hundred unidentified letters of your choosing, and a contributor reviews its high-confidence answers? That would show how much of the remaining set is tractable.
  2. Are there collections still to be entered where a first-pass suggestion, with abstention, would save contributors time?
  3. Does CSA hold reviewed identifications not yet merged into a CBDB release? They would make a clean, unseen test set.
  4. If useful, what output format would fit your workflow (for example, the CSA task CSV with a suggested 通訊人 and a confidence column)?
